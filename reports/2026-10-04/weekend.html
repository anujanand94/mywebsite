Everything physical starts here. Cars, bridges, pipelines, skyscrapers, soda cans, wind turbines — all need steel or aluminum. 3. How the Money Works

Revenue = tons shipped × price per ton. Both swing violently with the cycle — zero stickiness. Two costs decide everything: raw material (iron ore + coking coal for blast furnaces; scrap + electricity for electric-arc furnaces) and the fixed cost of keeping a furnace lit. The killer metric is the metal spread — selling price minus input cost. Scale helps, but furnace type matters more: Nucor operates primarily electric-arc furnace facilities, which flex output cheaply. Blast furnaces must run hot or bleed cash — like a jumbo jet that's uneconomic half-full.

4. The 4 Macro Drivers

Driver 1: Trade Policy / Tariffs

Mechanism: Section 232 tariffs are a price floor — they tax imports, letting domestic mills raise prices without losing share. Enters through price, the top line.
Now:The administration doubled steel and aluminum tariffs from 25% to 50%. Domestic HRC near $875. 2nd-order effect: Juniors cheer higher prices but miss the whipsaw — tariffs crush producers with cross-border footprints. Stelco idled Hamilton Works precisely because tariffs on Canadian steel killed demand.

Threshold: Any tariff rollback toward 25% — the floor cracks and the trade unwinds fast.

Driver 2: Construction & Infrastructure Demand

Mechanism: Non-residential construction and infrastructure spending drive ~40% of steel demand. Enters through volume — tons shipped.
Now: Demand is stabilizing after weakness; price hikes signal confidence. New capacity is being greenlit — plans for a large new steel mill in Iowa by Mesabi Metallics, tied to administration support, using electric-arc furnace technology.

2nd-order effect: New domestic capacity is bullish demand today but bearish pricing in 2-3 years — each new mill eventually floods the protected market.
Threshold: Architecture Billings Index back below 50 signals construction orders rolling over.

Driver 3: Interest Rates

Mechanism: Rates hit two ways — they raise discount rates (compressing multiples) and choke rate-sensitive end demand (autos, housing, capex). Enters via demand and multiples.
Now: Rate path uncertain; steel is deep-cyclical and low-multiple, so it de-rates less on rates than growth sectors but suffers more on demand destruction.
2nd-order effect: The real damage isn't the multiple — it's that high rates delay the customer's capex decision, so volume dries up a quarter before anyone models it.
Threshold: Auto SAAR dropping below 15M units — a direct demand warning.

Driver 4: China Export Flows & Global Overcapacity

Mechanism: China produces half the world's steel; when its domestic demand sags, exports flood global markets, crushing ex-US prices. Enters via global price and import pressure.
Now: Tariffs insulate the U.S., but global benchmarks stay soft on Chinese oversupply — hence the U.S. premium.
2nd-order effect: Cheap global steel pressures U.S. manufacturers who compete with imported finished goods — hurting steel's own customers.
Threshold: Chinese monthly steel exports above 10M tons signals a new dumping wave.

7. How to Value These Companies

Use EV/EBITDA, not P/E — cyclicals' earnings swing so wildly that P/E looks cheapest at the top (peak earnings) and most expensive at the bottom. Mid-cycle EV/EBITDA of 6-8x is typical; price-to-book backstops the downside. The classic junior mistake: buying at a 4x P/E near peak margins, thinking it's cheap, right before the spread collapses. Always normalize earnings to mid-cycle before you value — never extrapolate today's metal spread forever.

9. Risk Map

Risk 1: Tariff Reversal

A policy floor can vanish overnight. Transmission: remove the tariff → imports flood back → domestic prices fall toward global benchmark → metal spreads collapse → leveraged producers' earnings evaporate. Precedent: every prior Section 232 modification sent steel equities tumbling within days. Analysts note that if tariffs become an obstacle, the administration could lower rates back to 25%. Early warning: trade-deal headlines, exemption carve-outs for allies, or inflation politics pressuring the White House to ease.

Risk 2: Fixed-Cost Leverage Trap (Blast Furnaces)

Integrated mills must run hot — idling a blast furnace is slow and expensive. Transmission: demand falls → utilization drops below breakeven → fixed costs per ton explode → margins turn negative fast. Precedent: the 2015-16 downturn pushed U.S. integrated mills into losses and bankruptcies while EAFs stayed profitable. CLF's Stelco idled Hamilton Works indefinitely under exactly this pressure. Early warning: utilization trending toward 70%, inventory building at service centers.

Risk 3: Chinese Overcapacity Dumping

China's half-the-world output, when domestic demand sags, spills overseas below cost. Transmission: Chinese exports surge → global prices crater → import pressure and WTO cases rise → even protected U.S. prices feel psychological drag. Precedent: the 2015 Chinese export wave crushed global steel prices and triggered the original trade-case cascade. Early warning: Chinese monthly exports breaking above 10M tons, widening U.S.-vs-global price gap that invites circumvention through third countries.

Risk 4: Demand Air-Pocket Meets New Capacity

The cruelest combination: new mills ramp just as demand rolls over. Transmission: tariff-era profits spur capacity builds → new EAF mills like Mesabi Metallics' Iowa project come online → supply outruns demand → prices fall despite tariffs. Precedent: every steel boom ends when overbuilding meets a slowdown — the late-1990s mini-mill glut is textbook. Early warning: announced capacity additions accelerating while construction and auto indicators soften simultaneously.

13. Three Questions You Should Be Able to Answer

Q1: Why does a steel stock's P/E look lowest exactly when you should sell?
A: Because steel is deep-cyclical with operating leverage. At the cycle peak, metal spreads are widest, so earnings are maximized — a huge E in P/E makes the ratio look cheap. But peak margins can't last; the next move is spread compression and earnings collapse. That's why you value on mid-cycle EV/EBITDA or price-to-book. Buying a 4x "cheap" steel P/E at peak margins is the classic rookie trap that precedes the biggest drawdowns.

Q2: Why can a 50% tariff hurt a domestic steelmaker?
A: The obvious effect: tariffs raise domestic prices, helping U.S. mills. The missed second-order effect: producers with cross-border operations get stranded on the wrong side of the wall. CLF's Canadian Stelco idled Hamilton Works, citing elevated U.S. tariffs on Canadian steel constraining demand. So the same policy that lifts Nucor's clean domestic footprint simultaneously destroys demand for an integrated multinational's foreign plants. Footprint geography determines whether a tariff is a gift or a guillotine.

Q3: Bull vs bear on steel today?
A: Bull: tariffs floor prices near $875, utilization recovering, Nucor up 51% and Steel Dynamics up 38% YTD confirm momentum. Bear: new EAF capacity (Mesabi Iowa) plus any tariff rollback toward 25% collapses the premium, and rate-sensitive auto/construction demand could roll over. What flips the view: a tariff easing headline or utilization sliding below 75% turns the bull case bearish overnight.
