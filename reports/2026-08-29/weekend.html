1. Why This Industry Exists

Two customers who almost never stop paying: governments buying security, and airlines buying capacity. Nations fund militaries regardless of the economy; airlines expand fleets as global travel grows. Both require machines too complex and regulated for newcomers to build. A portfolio holds A&D for defensive, backlog-visible cash flows that keep compounding through recessions when little else does.

3. How the Money Works

Revenue splits into two engines. Original equipment (jets, missiles) is low-margin, capital-heavy, and lumpy. The real prize is aftermarket — spare parts and service on installed hardware for decades. The killer costs are engineering labor and titanium/composite inputs; scale helps only where a fleet is large enough to amortize development. Great businesses sell the razor at cost and own the blades forever. GE Aerospace provides durable growth through its dominant aircraft engine platform and high-margin aftermarket services business — like a printer maker who makes nothing on the printer and everything on ink.

4. The 4 Macro Drivers

Driver 1: Global Defense Budgets

Mechanism: Government appropriations flow directly into prime contractor revenue via multi-year contracts, insulating demand from the business cycle.
Now:Rising global security concerns continue to drive defense spending worldwide, with governments investing in combat vehicles, naval platforms, mission systems and ISR capabilities. NATO rearmament and Indo-Pacific tension underpin the tape. 2nd-order effect: Juniors watch topline; the money is in funded backlog conversion. Budgets authorize, but appropriations and outlays lag by years — cash arrives long after the headline.
Threshold: A U.S. continuing resolution or budget-cap deal that freezes new-start programs flips the thesis.

Driver 2: Commercial Air Traffic & Fleet Renewal

Mechanism: Passenger volume growth forces airlines to order narrowbodies, driving OEM production rates and, crucially, future aftermarket flying hours.
Now: Traffic above pre-pandemic levels keeps Airbus/Boeing backlogs at a decade-plus. Every delivered jet seeds 25 years of high-margin service revenue.
2nd-order effect: The obvious trade is OEM deliveries; the real leverage is shop visits. Engines flown harder need overhauls sooner — the aftermarket accelerates before deliveries do.
Threshold: A sustained drop in revenue-passenger-kilometers or airline deferrals signals the cycle is rolling over.

Driver 3: Interest Rates & Multiples

Mechanism: A&D cash flows are long-duration; higher discount rates compress the multiples on those distant aftermarket profits, de-rating the group.
Now: With rates elevated, long-duration industrials de-rated — part of why ITA lagged SPY by 16.75 points over six months despite solid earnings.
2nd-order effect: Beyond multiples, rates raise the cost of financing customer purchases — airlines lease jets, and pricier leases slow orders at the margin.
Threshold: A decisive Fed pivot re-rates the long-duration aftermarket names first and hardest.

Driver 4: Supply Chain & Input Costs

Mechanism: Titanium, castings, and skilled labor are bottlenecks; shortages cap production rates and squeeze fixed-price program margins.
Now:Geopolitical tensions, tariffs and trade policies may disrupt supply chains and increase costs, while labor shortages and inflation could affect production schedules and margins.

2nd-order effect: Juniors fear margin; the deeper risk is charges on fixed-price development contracts — cost overruns hit at 100% below the ceiling.
Threshold: Repeated program charges or a rate-cut deferral at a major OEM signals the constraint is structural.

7. How to Value These Companies

Use EV/EBITDA and forward P/E, but weight free-cash-flow yield most — A&D earnings are distorted by program accounting and pension noise, so cash is truth. Aftermarket-rich names (GE, RTX) earn premium multiples (20–38x); pure primes trade cheaper (15–18x) on growth doubts. The classic junior mistake: valuing OEM deliveries as the prize when the aftermarket annuity behind each delivery is where 70%+ of lifetime profit actually lives.

9. Risk Map

Risk 1: Fixed-Price Development Charges

A prime bids a fixed price on an unbuilt system; costs overrun and the contractor eats 100% above the ceiling. Transmission: multi-hundred-million charges crush a quarter's margin and cash. Precedent: Boeing's KC-46 tanker and defense fixed-price programs booked billions in cumulative losses across the early 2020s. Early warning: rising EAC adjustments and negative program margins buried in segment footnotes — the number management least wants discussed on the call.

Risk 2: Single Engine/Model Grounding

A technical fault forces a fleet grounding, halting deliveries and triggering airline compensation. Transmission: aftermarket revenue evaporates while inspection and penalty costs mount — a double hit to the highest-margin line. Precedent: the 737 MAX grounding cost Boeing ~$20bn and years of production. Early warning: an FAA airworthiness directive, incident clusters, or rising warranty reserves. One fault can erase a decade of aftermarket goodwill overnight.

Risk 3: Defense Budget Freeze / Continuing Resolution

Congress fails to pass appropriations, freezing new-start programs at prior-year levels and delaying awards. Transmission: book-to-bill slips below 1.0x, backlog stops growing, and multiples compress on stalled growth. Precedent: 2013 sequestration cut defense outlays and de-rated the primes for over a year. Early warning: budget-cap negotiations, a lapsed appropriation, or program deferrals in the President's request. The revenue is safe; the growth is what freezes.

Risk 4: Supply-Chain / Titanium Bottleneck

A critical input — titanium, castings, or skilled labor — becomes scarce, capping production rates below demand. Transmission: OEMs miss delivery targets, fixed costs under-absorb, and margins compress even with a full order book. Precedent: post-pandemic castings and labor shortages held Boeing and Airbus rates below plan for years despite record backlogs. Early warning: OEMs cutting monthly rate targets, or suppliers flagging lead-time extensions. Demand isn't the problem — the factory simply can't feed it.

13. Three Questions You Should Be Able to Answer

Q1: Why do OEM aircraft deliveries barely move the needle on profit?
A: Because the airframe or engine is sold near cost — sometimes below — to win the 25-year service stream behind it. The lifetime profit sits in spare parts and overhauls, not the delivery. GE's CFM engines make little on the sale but generate decades of high-margin shop visits. Juniors model delivery ramps and miss that a delivery is a cost of acquiring an annuity. The right question isn't "how many jets shipped" but "how many flying hours and shop visits did those jets seed."

Q2: Why did ITA lag SPY by 16.75 points over six months despite 21% earnings growth?
A: Multiples, not fundamentals. A&D cash flows are long-duration — value sits in distant aftermarket profits — so elevated discount rates compress those multiples hardest. The cap-weighted index also carries Boeing's drag. Earnings grew, but the de-rating overwhelmed it. The transmission: rates up → discount rate up → long-duration multiples compress → ITA de-rates even as backlog and revenue climb. When rates ease, the aftermarket-rich names re-rate first — the lag reverses.

Q3: Bull vs bear on A&D given today's macro?
A: Bull: record backlogs, firm defense budgets, traffic above pre-COVID, aftermarket accelerating — earnings up 21% YoY. Bear: valuations full on winners (GE ~38x), rate sensitivity, supply bottlenecks capping delivery ramps, and fixed-price charge risk. The evidence today favors selective bulls in aftermarket hybrids. What flips it: a budget freeze, a traffic rollover, or a rate-cut deferral at a major OEM.
