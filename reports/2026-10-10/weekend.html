1. Why This Industry Exists

People must eat, and increasingly they'd rather pay someone else to cook. Restaurants sell convenience, speed, and a small affordable treat — the "cheap luxury" that survives when bigger purchases get cut. A portfolio holds the category for its steady cash, pricing power, and brands that compound unit growth across decades.

3. How the Money Works

Two opposite models. Franchisors (MCD, QSR, YUM) are landlords: McDonald's is overwhelmingly franchised and collects royalties and rent from operators who run the restaurants day to day. That's high-margin, capital-light, inflation-protected rent. Company-operators (CMG, most SBUX) carry the kitchen: food (~30%) and labor (~25-30%) are the two costs that make or break margins. Scale helps both — buying power on food, brand pulls traffic. Great businesses own real estate and franchise it (MCD). Average ones chase units without unit economics. Think franchising as owning the toll booth, not driving the truck.

4. The 4 Macro Drivers

Driver 1: The Low-End Consumer

Mechanism: Demand. Restaurant traffic is discretionary — the first expense a stretched household cuts is eating out.
Now: The 24.5-point 6-month lag vs SPY reflects a market pricing a tired consumer. Even CMG is showing positive customer traffic but with profitability pressured by rising food and labor costs.

2nd-order effect: Trade-down. A weak consumer isn't all bad — diners shift from casual dining into QSR, so value leaders can gain share even as the pie shrinks. The loser is mid-tier fast-casual caught between.
Threshold: Two straight quarters of positive traffic (not just price) signals the bottom.

Driver 2: Food & Labor Cost Inflation

Mechanism: Costs. For company-operators these two lines are ~55-60% of sales; small moves swing margins hard.
Now: The persistent squeeze is exactly what's dragging CMG — Q2 2026 showed Chipotle rebuilding traffic while costs squeezed its profitability.

2nd-order effect: Menu pricing has a ceiling. Push price past wage growth and traffic collapses — you book revenue but lose customers permanently to the grocery aisle. Franchisors are insulated: their royalty rides the operator's topline, not its margin.
Threshold: Restaurant-level margin stabilizing while traffic stays positive = pricing power intact.

Driver 3: Interest Rates & Multiples

Mechanism: Multiples. Restaurants are long-duration unit-growth stories; higher discount rates compress the premium paid for future store openings.
Now: Higher-for-longer rates de-rated the group, hitting the richest multiples hardest — hence CMG's underperformance despite decent traffic.
2nd-order effect: Rates also raise franchisees' build cost. Fewer new units open, so even capital-light franchisors see unit growth slow — a lag most juniors miss.
Threshold: A sustained move toward rate cuts re-rates the highest-growth names first.

Driver 4: M&A / Consolidation

Mechanism: Multiples and sentiment. Depressed valuations turn laggards into targets.
Now:Chipotle's recent rebound was driven by speculative Starbucks takeover reports rather than fundamental changes.

2nd-order effect: Deal talk re-rates the whole peer group — lifted MCD and QSR on Oct. 8 too — by resetting the floor on "what these are worth."
Threshold: A confirmed bid (or denial) that validates or breaks the floor.

7. How to Value These Companies

Use EV/EBITDA and P/E for franchisors (clean, cash-rich, comparable) and EV/EBITDA plus unit-growth-adjusted P/E for operators. Franchisors deserve premium multiples (18-25x earnings) because royalty streams are annuities; operators trade cheaper on margin risk. The common junior mistake: valuing a franchisor and an operator on the same multiple. MCD's rent is worth far more per dollar than CMG's kitchen profit — never blend them.

9. Risk Map

Risk 1: Margin Squeeze from Food & Labor Spike

Costs that are ~55-60% of an operator's sales surge faster than menu pricing can follow. Transmission: compressed restaurant-level margins → earnings miss → multiple de-rating on a growth name. Precedent: CMG's 2026 slide, where Q2 showed Chipotle rebuilding traffic while costs squeezed profitability. Early warning: average wage growth outrunning menu price increases, and commodity baskets (beef, avocado, coffee) spiking. Franchisors escape this; company-operators wear it fully — the reason to own the landlord, not the tenant, mid-squeeze.

Risk 2: Consumer Trade-Down / Traffic Collapse

A stretched low-end stops eating out entirely. Transmission: negative traffic → operating deleverage (fixed rent/labor spread over fewer tickets) → margin and comp misses compound. Precedent: the group's 6-month -24.5-point lag vs SPY as the market priced a fatigued consumer. Early warning: traffic negative while check stays positive — meaning growth is pure price, not demand. The subtle part: trade-down helps QSR value leaders even as it hurts fast-casual, so direction depends on price tier.

Risk 3: Over-Discounting the Brand

Chasing traffic with deep value deals trains customers to wait for promotions, permanently lowering the profit per visit. Transmission: positive comps but falling average check → royalty base shrinks for franchisors, margins thin for operators → quality-of-earnings concern re-rates the stock. Precedent: value-war cycles that lifted traffic but gutted franchisee profit. Early warning: comps driven by transactions while average check declines quarter after quarter — the "winning share, losing money" trap most juniors misread as strength.

Risk 4: Failed or Phantom M&A

Deal speculation inflates a stock, then evaporates. Transmission: speculative pop → reversion to fundamentals when no bid materializes → volatility and sentiment damage. Precedent: the October 2026 CMG rally — no acquisition offer, transaction agreement, or formally announced process disclosed by either company. Early warning: huge volume on rumor (CMG traded ~271% above average) with no confirming filing. Never underwrite a thesis on a takeover report; underwrite the business beneath it.

13. Three Questions You Should Be Able to Answer

Q1: Why can two restaurant stocks with identical comps deserve wildly different multiples?
A: Because ownership model decides earnings quality. A franchisor like MCD collects royalties and rent from operators — a capital-light annuity immune to kitchen-cost swings. A company-operator like CMG wears food and labor directly. Identical comp growth means very different cash: the franchisor's dollar is cleaner and more durable, deserving 20x+; the operator's is cost-exposed. Blend them on one multiple and you'll overpay for risk or underpay for an annuity.

Q2: Why can a weak consumer be good for part of this sector?
A: Trade-down. When households stretch, they don't stop eating out — they shift from casual dining and premium fast-casual into value QSR. So a value franchisor can post positive traffic while the category shrinks. The transmission most juniors miss: aggregate "consumer weakness" headlines mask share gains at the bottom tier. The threshold is price point — MCD benefits, mid-tier fast-casual caught between gets squeezed from both sides. Read the tier, not the sector.

Q3: Bull vs bear on the group given today's macro?
A: Bull: the group lags SPY by 24.5 points over six months, valuations are cheap, M&A is validating the floor, and traffic is turning positive — CMG shows positive customer traffic and international scaling. Bear: profitability remains pressured by rising food and labor costs in a late-cycle, tired-consumer backdrop, and the CMG pop is speculative, not fundamental. What flips it: rate cuts plus two quarters of positive traffic with stable margins.
