Weekend Industry Master Class: Airlines

1. Why This Industry Exists

Airlines move people and cargo across distances too far to drive. Business travelers pay for speed, leisure flyers for access, shippers for time-sensitive freight. Demand is deeply tied to income and confidence. A portfolio holds airlines as a high-beta bet on consumer strength, cheap fuel, and disciplined capacity — a leveraged play on the economic cycle.

2. What's Happening Right Now

What happened: JETS sits at $28.98, down 1.26% over one month and 12.39% over three, lagging SPY by 1.54 and a brutal 16.74 points respectively. Yet six-month return is +20.10%. Delta and United both posted strong Q2 2026 earnings and raised or affirmed guidance despite rising jet fuel costs — United's decline despite beating suggests sentiment about future fuel, not weak fundamentals, is driving selling.

American told the Morgan Stanley Laguna crowd it "feels really good" about 16%-19% Q3 revenue growth, calling recent strength "durable."

Why it happened: Fuel is the swing factor. IATA sharply cut its 2026 industry profit outlook, citing an oil surge from Middle East tensions, warning higher fuel costs weigh heavily on earnings. Multiples compressed on fuel fear even as revenue records printed.

What it sets up: Q3 prints in mid-October are the referendum on whether the fuel tailwind held.

3. How the Money Works

Revenue = seats × load factor × yield, plus loyalty and cargo. Two costs decide everything: fuel (~30%) and labor (~30%). Net income fell year over year at United, driven by a $2.3 billion jump in fuel costs. Scale helps — hub density spreads fixed costs — but the real margin engine now is premium and loyalty. Delta attributes its confidence to strong, broad-based demand, particularly premium, loyalty, and corporate segments. Analogy: an airline is a hotel with a jet engine — mostly-fixed costs, and the last ten rooms sold are pure profit.

4. The 4 Macro Drivers

Driver 1: Jet Fuel Price (Crude + Crack Spread)

Mechanism: Fuel is ~30% of costs and flows straight to the P&L — a dollar move per gallon is billions.
Now:Spot jet fuel was back below $2.80 a gallon, far under the $4.30 United and roughly $4.00 American assumed for Q2 guidance, giving carriers a meaningful cost tailwind provided prices hold.

2nd-order effect: Juniors watch crude; the money is in the crack spread — refining margins can spike even when crude is flat, silently taxing airlines.
Threshold: Sustained jet fuel above $3.20/gal wipes the tailwind and forces fare hikes into softening demand.

Driver 2: Consumer & Corporate Demand

Mechanism: Airlines are a leveraged bet on discretionary income and business confidence. Demand cracks fast in downturns.
Now:IATA noted passenger demand remains relatively resilient, and air-travel demand remains upbeat despite oil volatility. Corporate and premium bookings are holding. 2nd-order effect: Not all demand is equal — front-cabin revenue is stickier and richer. A softening economy hits the back of the plane first, so total traffic can look fine while margins quietly bleed.
Threshold: Corporate bookings decelerating two straight months signals the premium mix is turning.

Driver 3: Capacity Discipline

Mechanism: Supply is the industry's own worst enemy. Add too many seats and yields collapse regardless of demand.
Now: Carriers are trimming unprofitable flying and shifting toward premium seats rather than raw growth. Discipline is intact.
2nd-order effect: The subtlety is where capacity lands. Low-cost carriers dumping seats on a route can destroy a legacy carrier's yield on that route even in a strong market.
Threshold: Industry ASM growth outpacing GDP-plus-travel-trend signals the discipline is breaking.

Driver 4: Interest Rates & Balance Sheet

Mechanism: Airlines carry heavy aircraft debt; rates drive interest expense and refinancing costs, while discount rates set the multiple.
Now:Delta further strengthened its investment-grade balance sheet through debt paydown and raised its dividend 15%.

2nd-order effect: Deleveraging quietly re-rates the equity — less debt means the enterprise value shifts to shareholders, so EPS grows faster than revenue.
Threshold: A carrier losing investment-grade rating spikes its financing cost and caps the multiple.

7. How to Value These Companies

Use EV/EBITDAR — it capitalizes aircraft leases and strips out debt distortions, letting you compare an owner versus a lessee fairly. P/E misleads because leverage and fuel hedging swing net income wildly. Typical ranges: 4–6x EV/EBITDAR trough-to-peak. The most common junior mistake: anchoring on a low P/E and calling an airline "cheap" while ignoring the debt in enterprise value — a leveraged carrier's equity can be expensive even at 5x earnings.

9. Risk Map

Risk 1: Fuel Spike Into Fixed Fares

Fuel is ~30% of costs and moves faster than fares can reprice. A geopolitical shock spikes crude, crack spreads widen, and the cost lands immediately while contracts and competition delay fare hikes. Airlines may attempt to pass fuel costs to consumers, but competitive pressures and weakening consumer spending could limit their ability to do so. Precedent: 2008 oil at $147 bankrupted carriers. Early warning: crack spreads widening with fares flat.

Risk 2: Capacity Overbuild

When times are good, everyone orders jets. Seats arrive years later — often into a weaker market — collapsing yields industry-wide. It's the classic airline suicide pact: rational for one carrier, ruinous collectively. Precedent: post-2000 and 2001 overcapacity drove a decade of losses and consolidation. Transmission: excess ASMs crush PRASM, margins compress, multiples de-rate. Early warning: industry ASM growth running above demand trend for two consecutive quarters, especially low-cost carriers flooding legacy hubs.

Risk 3: Demand Air Pocket (Recession)

Air travel is discretionary; a confidence shock empties planes fast, and high fixed costs mean profits vanish overnight. Corporate travel — the margin engine — cuts first and returns last. Transmission: falling load factors and yields hit revenue while costs stay fixed, so operating leverage works violently in reverse. Precedent: 2020 COVID zeroed demand; 2008-09 corporate travel collapse. Early warning: sequential decline in corporate and premium bookings while total traffic still looks healthy.

Risk 4: Balance-Sheet / Refinancing Shock

Airlines carry heavy aircraft debt. If rates rise or a downgrade hits during a downturn, refinancing costs spike and cash burn forces distressed decisions — the most leveraged carriers first. Transmission: higher interest expense and lost investment-grade status cap the multiple and drain liquidity. Precedent: multiple legacy Chapter 11 filings in the 2000s stemmed from debt loads meeting demand shocks. Early warning: widening credit spreads on a carrier's bonds and negative rating-agency outlooks.

13. Three Questions You Should Be Able to Answer

Q1: Why can an airline post record revenue yet see net income fall — and why doesn't that mean the business is broken?
A: Because fuel is a massive, volatile cost that hits the P&L independent of revenue. United's adjusted EPS of $1.99 beat by roughly 6%, yet net income fell year over year, driven by a $2.3 billion jump in fuel costs. The revenue engine and demand are healthy; the earnings decline is a fuel event, not a demand event. That's why the stock's fuel-fear discount can be an opportunity.

Q2: Fuel prices are falling — what's the second-order effect most people miss?
A: Cheap fuel doesn't just lift margins; it tempts the whole industry to add capacity, which eventually crushes yields. The transmission: low fuel → strong profits → aircraft orders and route additions → seats flood the market in 12–24 months → PRASM falls → margins compress despite cheap fuel. Spot jet fuel below $2.80, far under the $4.30 United assumed, is a tailwind provided prices hold. The discipline question outlasts the fuel question.

Bear: IATA sharply cut its 2026 profit outlook citing an oil surge from Middle East tensions.
