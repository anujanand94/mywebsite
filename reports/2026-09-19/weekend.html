1. Why This Industry Exists

Every business runs on software it can't build itself: databases, CRM, security, workflow. Rather than buy a program once, companies now rent it monthly — like leasing a delivery fleet instead of buying trucks. Customers keep paying because switching breaks everything downstream. Portfolios hold it for durable, compounding, high-margin recurring revenue that grows faster than GDP.

3. How the Money Works

Revenue is subscription: booked once, billed forever, with net revenue retention above 100% meaning existing customers spend more each year — a landlord who raises rent while tenants never leave. The two costs that decide profitability: sales & marketing (customer acquisition) and cloud hosting/compute. Scale helps enormously — code written once serves millions at near-zero marginal cost, so gross margins hit 75–85%. Great businesses (ServiceNow) show falling S&M as a share of revenue while retention rises; average ones buy growth. Net revenue retention is the gold standard.

4. The 4 Macro Drivers

Driver 1: Interest Rates / Discount Rates

Mechanism: Software profits arrive years out, so valuation is long-duration. Higher rates raise the discount rate, shrinking the present value of distant cash flows — multiples compress hardest here.
Now: Rate-cut expectations underpinned the 3-month +16.94pt outperformance vs SPY; falling discount rates re-inflated the priciest names.
2nd-order effect: Juniors watch the headline cut; the real move is the terminal-rate re-rating — where rates settle, not the next 25bp. Growth software leads when the market prices the plateau.
Threshold: 10-year Treasury breaking back above ~4.75% reverses the multiple tailwind.

Driver 2: Hyperscaler Capex Cycle

Mechanism: Cloud infrastructure demand is set by Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Oracle capex budgets. Their spend is downstream software's revenue.

Threshold: Net new ARR growth decelerating two straight quarters across bellwethers.

Driver 4: AI Monetization

Mechanism: AI enters both as a cost (compute/inference bills) and a demand driver (new products, usage-based upsell).
Now:Oracle hosts AI workloads for OpenAI, Meta, Nvidia and Microsoft simultaneously — AI compute is monetizing fastest at the infrastructure layer. 2nd-order effect: The obvious trade is buying AI winners; the subtle one is spotting whom inference costs crush — SaaS firms bundling AI free while paying rising compute bills see gross margins quietly erode.
Threshold: A marquee SaaS name cutting gross-margin guidance on AI costs.

7. How to Value These Companies

Use EV/Sales and EV/FCF, not P/E — many reinvest profits away, so earnings understate value; recurring revenue and cash generation are the true engine. Rule of 40 (growth + margin ≥ 40%) screens quality. Typical EV/Sales: 6–20x depending on growth. The most common junior mistake: paying a high-growth multiple on a decelerating name — the multiple compresses faster than revenue grows, and you lose both ways.

9. Risk Map

Risk 1: Multiple Compression from Rising Rates

Long-duration cash flows get discounted harder when rates rise, hammering the priciest names first. Transmission: discount rate up → present value down → multiple contracts even with flat fundamentals. Precedent: 2022's rate shock cut software multiples in half despite growing revenue. Early warning: 10-year yield breaking higher while forward EV/Sales stays elevated — the setup for a violent de-rating.

Risk 2: AI Compute Cost Squeeze

SaaS firms bundling AI features pay rising inference bills while charging customers little extra. Transmission: compute cost up → gross margin down → Rule of 40 breaks → multiple compresses. No clean precedent yet — this is the new risk. Early warning: a bellwether cutting gross-margin guidance and blaming AI/hosting costs. Watch the gap between AI feature launches and actual monetization.

Risk 3: Platform Consolidation Killing Point Solutions

Customers standardize on fewer vendors, starving single-product firms. Transmission: budget reallocation → mid-tier ARR shrinks → growth stalls → valuation collapses. Precedent: numerous 2023–24 point-solution SaaS names crushed as buyers consolidated onto Microsoft and platform vendors. Early warning: decelerating net new logos at smaller vendors while platforms report accelerating module attach — the money migrating in real time.

Risk 4: Neocloud / Customer-Concentration Bust

GPU renters live on a few mega-contracts and heavy debt-funded capex. Transmission: one customer leaves or AI demand cools → utilization drops → depreciation strands returns → equity impairs. Precedent: telecom/dot-com overbuild busts follow the same capex-ahead-of-demand pattern. Early warning: contracted backlog growth stalling while capex and debt keep climbing, or a top-three customer non-renewing.

13. Three Questions You Should Be Able to Answer

Q1: Why can a software company grow revenue yet destroy value?
A: Because growth bought with heavy S&M or debt-funded capex doesn't compound if retention is weak. If NRR sits below 100%, the company loses more from churn than it gains from upsell, so each new customer just replaces a lost one. Value comes from durable, expanding relationships — not gross bookings. A neocloud growing revenue 80% while burning cash and depending on one customer is fragile, not valuable.

Q2: How do falling rates and rising AI costs pull software in opposite directions?
A: Falling rates lower the discount rate, inflating long-duration multiples — the tailwind behind software's 17-point lead over SPY this quarter. But rising AI inference costs compress gross margins, breaking the Rule of 40. So a name can see its multiple expand on rates while its fundamentals quietly deteriorate on compute bills. The two forces net out per-name: infrastructure toll-collectors win both; margin-squeezed SaaS wins one, loses the other.

Q3: Bull vs bear on software today, given the macro?
A: Bull: AI spend is flowing through the income statement, rates are easing, and backlogs like Oracle's $638B RPO signal years of revenue. Bear: backlogs are growing faster than revenue, meaning depreciation hits margins before cash arrives, and valuations already price perfection. What flips it: Q3 prints showing backlog converting to expanding margins confirms the bull; any capex-guidance cut confirms the bear.
