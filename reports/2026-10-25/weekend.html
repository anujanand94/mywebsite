1. Why This Industry Exists

Oil and gas get pulled out of the ground in Texas and North Dakota but burned in cities and shipped abroad. Midstream owns the roads in between — pipelines, storage tanks, processing plants, export docks. They charge a toll on every molecule, like a turnpike. Portfolios hold them for fat, contracted, inflation-linked cash yields that barely flinch with commodity prices.

3. How the Money Works

Revenue is a toll: fee-per-barrel or fee-per-Mcf under multi-year take-or-pay contracts, often with inflation escalators. Stickiness is extreme — customers pay whether they ship or not. The two costs that decide profitability: interest expense (these are leveraged, capital-heavy assets) and maintenance capex. Scale absolutely helps — a second pipeline in the same corridor costs little incrementally but doubles tolls. DT Midstream posted Q2 net income of $112 million and Adjusted EBITDA of $305 million — a ~35% margin. Think highway operator: build once, collect tolls for decades.

7. How to Value These Companies

Use EV/EBITDA and distribution (or FCF) yield, not P/E. Why: EBITDA strips out the huge, non-cash depreciation on long-lived pipes that distorts earnings, and yield captures the contracted cash these are actually bought for. Typical range: 8–11x EV/EBITDA. The most common junior mistake — anchoring on P/E and calling a 25x pipe "expensive," ignoring that depreciation makes earnings understate true cash generation.

9. Risk Map

Risk 1: Rate-Driven Multiple Compression

A bond-proxy's worst enemy. Rising long rates lift discount rates on contracted cash flows and raise floating-rate interest expense, squeezing both multiple and margin simultaneously. In 2015 and again during 2022's hiking cycle, MLPs de-rated sharply as yields on "safe" Treasuries competed with distributions. Early warning: the 10-year breaking higher while distribution yields fail to rise in step — the spread compressing is the tell before the price drop.

Risk 2: Volume Depletion from Producer Capital Discipline

Gathering systems need new wells constantly; existing wells deplete. When E&Ps prioritize buybacks over drilling — as they have post-2020 — gathering volumes quietly erode even at healthy commodity prices. The 2016 Permian slowdown gutted gatherer cash flows. Early warning: producer capex guidance cut while rig counts drift lower, preceding volume declines by two to three quarters.

Risk 3: Leverage & Distribution Cuts

Capital-heavy balance sheets plus a growth-capex binge can force a distribution cut when cash flow dips. Kinder Morgan's 2015 75% distribution slash vaporized equity value overnight and shattered the sector's "safe income" reputation. Transmission: coverage falls below 1.0x → agencies threaten downgrade → management cuts payout → yield investors flee. Early warning: coverage ratio drifting toward 1.0x while net debt/EBITDA climbs above 4.5x.

Risk 4: Regulatory & Permitting Shock

Pipelines live or die by FERC approvals and court challenges. The cancelled Constitution and Atlantic Coast pipelines torched billions in sunk capital after years of permitting. A single adverse ruling can strand a half-built asset. Transmission: permit denial → capex written off → growth outlook cut → multiple de-rates. Early warning: contested permits, hostile state regulators, or FERC composition shifts signaling tougher approvals.

13. Three Questions You Should Be Able to Answer

Q1: Why do midstream firms report DCF instead of EPS, and why can earnings mislead?
A: Pipelines carry enormous non-cash depreciation on assets that physically last decades longer than their accounting schedule. That depreciation crushes reported earnings while actual cash keeps flowing. DCF adds depreciation back and subtracts only true maintenance capex, revealing cash available for distributions. A junior looking at AM's 24.49 P/E calls it expensive; the cash yield tells the real, cheaper story.

Q2: How does a falling rig count hurt a gatherer two quarters before it shows in revenue?
A: Gathering fees depend on flowing volumes. Existing wells deplete 30-50% in year one. New wells replace that decline — so when rigs drop, you don't see it immediately because current wells still flow. But as depletion outruns new connections, throughput rolls over roughly two to three quarters later. The transmission: rig cut → fewer completions → depletion wins → volumes fall → fee revenue drops.
