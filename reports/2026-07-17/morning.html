8. Concept Unlocked

Earnings yield vs. bond yield- What it is (plain English): Earnings yield is a company's earnings divided by its price — the inverse of the P/E. You compare it to the yield on a "risk-free" bond to ask: am I being paid enough extra to own the stock instead of the Treasury? - The mechanism: When bond yields rise, the bond becomes a more attractive alternative, so investors demand a higher earnings yield from stocks — which means a lower P/E. High-multiple (low earnings-yield) stocks are hit hardest because they have the least cushion above the bond. - Today's live example: With the 30y at 5.098%, a stock like AVGO at ~34x forward carries an earnings yield near 2.9% — below the long bond. That negative spread is why a rising 30y mechanically pulls the multiple down; a staple at ~20x (5% earnings yield) still clears the hurdle, which is exactly why XLP (+2.80%) is bid and XLK (-2.24%) is sold. - When to use this: Reach for it whenever long yields are rising into an expensive tape — it tells you which stocks de-rate first and by how much.

Capital-light compounder- What it is (plain English): A business that grows earnings without needing to pour cash into factories or equipment — it reinvests a little capital and earns a lot back, year after year. - The mechanism: Because it doesn't sink money into physical capex, almost all its profit converts to free cash flow, and high returns on the little capital it does deploy let earnings compound at high rates for a long time. - Today's live example: Constellation Software (CSU.TO +5.75% to C$2,825.42) led the TSX on a red-tape day — a software roll-up that buys cash-generative niche businesses and reinvests the proceeds. In a rotation away from capex-heavy semis, the market rewards the mirror-image profile: capital-light, cash-now compounding. - When to use this: In a capex-peak rotation — the market pays up for businesses that don't need to build, precisely when it punishes the ones that do.

The idea: Why a bear steepener on benign inflation is more dangerous to tech than a growth scare.

The surface understanding: "Yields went up, so stocks went down." True but useless — it doesn't tell you which stocks or why the character of the move matters.

The level beneath: A stock's price is the present value of future cash flows, discounted back at a rate tied to long yields. Mega-cap tech has most of its cash flows far in the future, so a change in the long-end discount rate moves its value far more than a company earning cash today — that's duration in equities.
