Weekend Industry Master Class: Regulated Utilities & Independent Power

Monday, October 12, 2026 | Mentored by a 30-Year MD

1. Why This Industry Exists

Electricity is the one bill nobody skips — hospitals, homes, and now AI data centers all need it, every second. Regulated utilities own the wires and plants and earn a government-blessed return on them. Independent power producers (IPPs) sell generation into markets. Portfolios hold them for bond-like dividends, inflation pass-through, and now, structural demand growth.

2. What's Happening Right Now

What happened: The group split in two. Regulated names bled — NEE −5.99%, DUK −2.32%, SO −1.15% over one month — while IPPs ran: VST +9.01%, CEG +4.68%. The group averaged +0.85% on the month but lagged SPY by 1.27pts, and trails badly over six months (−19.59pts).

Why it happened:PJM capacity prices jumped from $28.92 to $329.17 per MW-day over roughly two years. That cash flows straight to merchant generators (VST, CEG), not rate-capped utilities. Meanwhile sticky long rates compressed the long-duration regulated names, whose fixed returns look bond-like when discount rates stay high.

What it sets up: Q3 earnings (late October) test whether data-center load is signing real contracts or just clogging queues — the catalyst for the next leg in both camps.

3. How the Money Works

Regulated utilities earn a fixed percentage return on their asset base ("rate base"). Build more poles and wires, earn more — like a landlord whose rent is set by a board but who can always add rooms. Revenue is bulletproof; the costs that decide profitability are interest expense (they're debt machines) and the allowed return the regulator grants. Scale helps via cheaper capital. IPPs like Vistra are opposite: revenue swings with power prices, so a hot summer or tight capacity auction is pure upside. Great operators own low-cost generation (nuclear) when prices spike.

4. The 4 Macro Drivers

Driver 1: Long-Term Interest Rates

Mechanism: Utilities are the most bond-like equities — fixed allowed returns discounted far into the future. Higher rates lift discount rates and compress those long-duration multiples.
Now: Sticky long rates explain the regulated de-rate: NEE −14.92% over six months, lagging SPY by 28.97pts. The market repriced their "safe yield" against higher risk-free bonds.
2nd-order effect: Juniors miss the refinancing drag — utilities roll enormous debt annually, so each quarter at higher coupons quietly erodes EPS even if the stock stabilizes.
Threshold: 10-year yield breaking below 3.75% re-rates the whole regulated complex.

Driver 2: Data-Center Load Growth

Mechanism: AI compute is the first real demand shock in two decades, lifting both rate-base investment (utilities) and power prices (IPPs). Data centers are projected to account for 33% of all US electricity load growth between 2024 and 2030.

Now:Summer peak load in PJM's Dominion zone hit 23,905 MW in 2025, 23% higher than 2019. This is why CEG and VST ran. 2nd-order effect: The queue is not the contract. Some utilities with stricter interconnection rules saw large-load queues shrink by 50% or more.

Threshold: Signed take-or-pay PPAs, not interconnection requests, confirm the thesis.

Driver 3: Capacity Market Pricing (PJM)

Mechanism: Capacity auctions pay generators to stay available. Prices exploded as reserves tightened — pure margin for merchant fleets.
Now:PJM capacity prices rose from $28.92 to $329.17 per MW-day, a roughly 10x move flowing to CEG/VST, invisible to rate-capped utilities. 2nd-order effect: Political backlash. Those auction costs hit consumer bills — US electricity prices reached 19 cents/kWh at end-2025, 27% above 2019 — inviting regulators to intervene and cap the windfall.
Threshold: FERC or state price caps on capacity auctions would gut the IPP thesis overnight.

Driver 4: Regulatory Rate-Case Outcomes

Mechanism: The allowed ROE a commission grants is the single most important number for a regulated utility — it sets the earnings ceiling.
Now: Rising rates pressure commissions to raise allowed ROEs, but ratepayer affordability (bills up 27%) pushes the other way. Tension keeps regulated multiples capped.
2nd-order effect: Constructive jurisdictions (Florida for NEE/Duke) quietly compound; hostile ones (parts of the Midwest) see capex deferred, slowing rate-base growth — the real long-term value engine.
Threshold: A string of ROE awards below 9.5% signals a de-rating regime.

6. Company Case Studies

Case Study 1: Constellation Energy (CEG) — The nuclear landlord to AI

Business: Largest US nuclear fleet, selling carbon-free baseload into merchant markets and increasingly via long-term PPAs to hyperscalers. Revenue is power price × volume; the key cost is fixed plant operations. At scale, nuclear's near-zero marginal cost means every dollar of price spike drops to margin.
Moat: You cannot build a new nuclear plant to compete — permitting and capital are prohibitive. CEG owns irreplaceable 24/7 clean megawatts exactly when AI needs them. The moat is widening as data centers pay premiums for firm clean power.
Macro Linkage: Drivers 2 and 3 hit hardest. Data-center demand and capacity pricing are pure tailwinds — CEG's existing fleet captures spikes with zero new capex. PJM's Dominion zone serves the largest concentration of data centers in the world, directly in CEG's wheelhouse.
Watch: (1) Contracted PPA volume with hyperscalers — signals locked-in cash flow; currently expanding. (2) Nuclear capacity factor — above 94% means the fleet is monetizing every available hour. Both confirm the thesis is real, not narrative.
Risk: A single extended nuclear outage removes irreplaceable supply and margin simultaneously. Early warning: rising forced-outage rates or NRC findings at a flagship plant.
Valuation: Trades on EV/EBITDA, now premium to peers after +16.42% three-month run. Fair-to-rich — priced for PPA execution that must now deliver.

Case Study 2: NextEra Energy (NEE) — The de-rated growth utility

Business: Hybrid — regulated Florida Power & Light (bulletproof rate base) plus the world's largest renewables developer. FPL earns on asset base; the development arm sells contracted clean energy. Key cost is interest expense, given the debt-heavy build. Scale lowers capital cost versus smaller developers.
Moat: Development scale and a constructive Florida regulator. NEE builds renewables cheaper than anyone and recovers costs reliably. But the moat erodes at the edges when rising rates make its growth premium harder to finance and justify.
Macro Linkage: Driver 1 dominates. As the most rate-sensitive growth utility, NEE's −14.92% six-month slide (−28.97pts vs SPY) is a pure discount-rate story. Its renewable development pipeline is long-duration cash flow — exactly what higher rates punish most severely.
Watch: (1) Renewable backlog additions — signals multi-year earnings; still growing. (2) FPL allowed ROE in next rate case — the regulated earnings ceiling. A constructive award re-rates the stock; a cut confirms the bear case.
Risk: Tax-credit policy reversal would strand the renewables economics. Early warning: legislative moves to phase out clean-energy credits faster than expected.
Valuation: Trades on P/E premium to utility peers. After the de-rate, screening cheap relative to history — a rate-cut call in equity form.

Case Study 3: Vistra (VST) — The capacity-auction windfall

Business: Merchant generator with a mixed gas, nuclear, and retail fleet concentrated in Texas (ERCOT) and PJM. Revenue is wholesale power plus capacity payments; profitability hinges on spark spreads and auction clears. Owning dispatchable generation lets Vistra sell into scarcity peaks at premium prices.
Moat: Fleet location and dispatchability. Vistra can turn on when prices spike — a structural edge in tightening grids. The moat widens as reserve margins shrink, but it is cyclical, not permanent, unlike nuclear scarcity.
Macro Linkage: Driver 3 is the engine. The 10x capacity-price move drove VST +9.01% on the month (+6.89pts vs SPY). Vistra monetizes tightness directly, with no regulator capping the upside — the mirror image of NEE's rate-bound model.
Watch: (1) Forward capacity auction clears — the forward earnings signal; elevated now. (2) ERCOT reserve margin — tighter means higher scarcity pricing. Both currently favorable; a reversal flags peak.
Risk: Political capacity-price caps or a mild weather year collapses the windfall. Early warning: FERC complaints about consumer bills or regulatory noise on auction design.
Valuation: EV/EBITDA, elevated after the run but EBITDA itself is cyclically high — the classic merchant trap of looking cheap at the peak.

7. How to Value These Companies

Value regulated names on P/E and premium-to-rate-base-growth, because earnings are a legislated function of asset base — dividend yield relative to the 10-year Treasury is the real anchor. Value IPPs on EV/EBITDA, since capital structure and merchant volatility distort earnings. The most common junior mistake: valuing a merchant generator on peak-cycle EBITDA as if it were recurring. Capacity windfalls are cyclical; capitalize them at a trough multiple, not a utility multiple.

9. Risk Map

Risk 1: Capacity-Price Political Cap

A 10x PJM capacity spike flowed to generators but hit consumer bills hard. When voters feel it — prices already 27% above 2019 — regulators intervene and cap auction prices. Transmission: merchant EBITDA collapses, multiples compress as the "windfall" proves temporary. Precedent: every prior merchant super-cycle ended in re-regulation. Early warning: FERC dockets reviewing capacity-market design, state AGs filing affordability complaints. This is the single biggest threat to the CEG/VST trade that just ran.

Risk 2: Data-Center Demand Disappointment

Interconnection queues are inflated with speculative requests. The data-center frenzy could lead to an unnecessary buildout like the turn of the century, when industries massively overestimated internet power needs, leaving utilities and ratepayers on the hook for underutilized assets. Transmission: utilities over-build rate base, regulators disallow recovery, IPPs face oversupply. Precedent: 2000-2001 merchant-plant bust bankrupted a generation of IPPs. Early warning: large load queues shrinking by 50% or more as tariffs weed out speculation.

Risk 3: Hostile Rate-Case Regime

When affordability dominates politics, commissions cut allowed ROEs and deny capex recovery. Transmission: the rate-base growth engine stalls, EPS growth evaporates, the stock re-rates to a bond. Precedent: periods of ROE compression in the 2010s turned growth utilities into yield traps. Early warning: a cluster of awards below 9.5%, disallowed storm costs, or populist utility-commission elections. This risk runs opposite the demand tailwind — great assets in a bad jurisdiction still underperform.

Risk 4: Refinancing Shock

Utilities are the most leveraged corporates outside banks, rolling huge debt annually. Sustained high rates mean each refinancing lifts interest expense, draining EPS beneath steady revenue. Transmission: coverage ratios weaken, dividends get questioned, credit downgrades raise capital cost further — a doom loop. Precedent: highly levered utilities cut dividends in past rate spikes. Early warning: FFO/debt slipping below 13%, rating-agency negative outlooks, dividend-payout ratios creeping above 70%.

11. Structural Themes

Theme 1: AI as the First Demand Shock in 20 Years

After two flat decades, load is growing again. From 2020 through 2026, electricity consumption is expected to grow 1.7% per year, led by data centers. This is accelerating because AI racks draw 50 to 100 kW each versus 5 to 10 kW for traditional enterprise. Winners: nuclear-heavy generators and utilities in data-center hubs. Losers: utilities without load growth. Position before consensus by owning firm-clean-power owners while the market still debates whether demand is real.

Theme 2: Power Scarcity as the Binding Constraint

The bottleneck has shifted from demand to supply. Power scarcity is now the primary constraint, with Northern Virginia grid-connection wait times exceeding seven years. This hands pricing power to anyone who already owns dispatchable or baseload capacity. Winners: existing fleet owners. Losers: new entrants and ratepayers. Position by favoring owned generation over development pipelines — in a scarcity regime, the incumbent fleet is the scarce asset.

13. Three Questions You Should Be Able to Answer

Q1: Why did IPPs soar while regulated utilities fell this year, if both are "utilities"?
A: They're opposite business models. Regulated utilities earn a capped return on rate base — a bond that got repriced by higher discount rates, hence NEE −14.92%. IPPs sell merchant power and capacity; the 10x PJM capacity spike flowed straight to their margin, hence VST +9.01%. One is rate-sensitive and capped; the other is price-exposed and uncapped. Calling them one sector hides the single most important distinction in the group today.

Q2: Why is a bigger interconnection queue not automatically bullish?
A: Because a queue request is free optionality, not a contract. Developers file speculatively across multiple utilities for the same project. Utilities with stricter rules saw queues shrink 50% or more once they imposed tariffs. The real signal is a signed take-or-pay PPA. The transmission most juniors miss: utilities that build rate base against phantom demand face disallowed cost recovery later — the exact 2001 merchant-bust mechanism replaying.

Q3: Bull vs bear on the group given today's macro?
A: Bull: a genuine demand super-cycle — data centers drive 33% of US load growth to 2030 — plus eventual rate cuts that re-rate regulated names. Bear: capacity windfalls are peak-cycle and politically vulnerable to caps as bills hit 27% above 2019, while sticky rates keep punishing regulated multiples. What flips it: signed hyperscaler PPAs confirm demand (bull), or a FERC capacity-price cap confirms the windfall was temporary (bear).
